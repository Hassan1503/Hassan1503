Md Hassanuzzaman

PhD Candidate · Machine learning for physiological signals

I build methods that turn raw physiological recordings into reliable clinical decisions. My thesis develops automated screening for congenital heart disease from heart sounds, designed to hold up on inexpensive stethoscopes in settings where echocardiography is out of reach. Alongside it I work on multimodal foundation models that learn shared representations across ECG, PPG and other clinical signals.

Research

My work runs along one line: from the instrument that records a physiological signal, through the data it produces and the tools that make that data usable, to the models trained on it. Each stage left something behind that other people can use — a device, a dataset, a toolbox, a model.

  1. 2019 — 2021

    Device

    A wearable ECG for continuous monitoring

    TnR Lab, North South University

    A three-lead ECG belt that streams over Wi-Fi to a server, with filtering and interval analysis in MATLAB and a companion app showing the live trace and heart rate, alongside atrial-fibrillation and fetal-heart-rate models. I designed the analog front-end. It began as my BSc thesis, became a conference paper, and reached the final of the IEEE Region 10 Humanitarian Technology Project Competition.

    doi:10.1109/R10-HTC47129.2019.9042478

  2. 2022 — 2025

    Data

    Paediatric congenital heart disease from heart sounds

    BIMS Lab, United International University, with the National Heart Foundation Hospital & Research Institute and Bangladesh Shishu Hospital & Institute

    Echocardiography is costly and scarce in exactly the places where congenital heart disease goes undiagnosed, so we asked whether a digital stethoscope and a deep model could screen children instead. Leading a two-member clinical team, I designed the collection protocol and gathered more than 3,000 simultaneous ECG and phonocardiogram recordings from around 700 patients across two hospitals.

    What that dataset supported: a transformer-based 1D-CNN that recognises the disease from raw heart sounds (0.92 accuracy, 0.96 AUC on 484 patients); a study fixing five seconds as the minimum segment length for reliable classification, using RMSSD and zero-crossing rate as quality gates (93.7% on five-second segments); and, with Monash University, a deep and handcrafted feature-fusion model evaluated on 751 subjects. Funded by the Institute of Advanced Research, UIU.

    doi:10.1109/EMBC40787.2023.10340370 doi:10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3573870 doi:10.1016/j.compbiomed.2025.110993 doi:10.22489/CinC.2023.146

  3. 2024 — present

    Toolbox

    MPT, the Multimodal Physiology Toolbox

    Kamaleswaran Lab, Duke University

    MPT derives cardiorespiratory physiomarkers from ECG, PPG, respiratory and arterial blood-pressure waveforms. I optimised it for CPU execution and added multithreaded parallel extraction, which cut feature-extraction time by about 70% on long recordings, and added GPU paths for 24-hour recordings.

    I also built its web application: load multimodal waveforms, process them, inspect the detected fiducial points, and export a table of more than 200 physiomarkers ready for model training. MPT is registered as a Duke University technology (software copyright, Ref. T-008998, September 2025).

    doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-8419239/v1

  4. 2025 — present

    Model

    CardioFM, a multimodal foundation model for ECG and PPG

    Kamaleswaran Lab, Duke University

    CardioFM is a self-supervised foundation model that learns a joint representation of the electrocardiogram and the photoplethysmogram, using cross-modal attention between the two signals and a vector-quantised tokeniser. It is pretrained on multi-site tertiary-hospital recordings from intensive-care and perioperative settings, with public ambulatory and wearable datasets held out for evaluation.

    The aim is one model that transfers across predictive tasks — cardiovascular disease classification, physiological estimation such as pulse arrival time — rather than a separate architecture for each. I designed the architecture, built the distributed multi-GPU training pipeline, and lead the eight-member team working on it. The manuscript is under review.

    doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-9652631/v1

Alongside

  1. Skin-tone bias in skin-cancer models

    Intelligent Systems Laboratory, University of Bristol, with Dr James Pope · 2024

    Open-source skin-cancer datasets come mostly from lighter-skinned populations. On an ISIC subset carrying tone labels we trained classifiers on imbalanced and balanced data and measured disparate impact by skin tone: 0.58 and 0.68, both below the 0.80 fairness threshold, which suggests rebalancing alone does not remove the bias. I ran the Grad-CAM analysis used to probe what the models attend to. Funded by the Jean Golding Institute.

    doi:10.48550/arXiv.2410.06385

  2. Histology and psoriasis image segmentation

    Lynch Lab, King’s College London (remote) · 2021 — 2022

    Semantic segmentation of hair follicles and sebaceous and eccrine glands in skin-biopsy histology with a ResNet encoder (IoU above 90%), and body and psoriatic-plaque segmentation with a feature-pyramid network on an SE-ResNeXt50 encoder (IoU 94%, F-score 97%).

  3. Physiological sensing from a smartphone

    TnR Lab, North South University · 2019 — 2021

    Blood-glucose regression from fingertip-video PPG, denoised with a Gaussian filter over asymmetric least squares, reaching a standard error of prediction of 17.02 mg/dL; and a crowdsourced traveller-assistant app that builds topological maps of routes and buildings from accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer and GPS data, where I was responsible for sensor noise reduction.

    doi:10.3390/app11020618 doi:10.3390/s21216969
